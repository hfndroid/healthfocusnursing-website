What is an NDIS continence assessment? A complete guide

Who conducts one, what happens on the day, which budget pays for it, and how to arrange one in Melbourne or Geelong.

Health Focus Nursing · Updated 21 September 2026 · 6 min read

If a bladder or bowel condition is affecting daily life, the NDIS will usually want a formal assessment before it funds continence supports. That requirement catches a lot of people off guard, particularly families who have been managing quietly for months and assumed products would simply be covered.

This guide covers what the assessment actually is, who is qualified to carry one out, what happens during the visit, what ends up in the report, and which part of an NDIS plan pays for it. It is written for participants and families, but coordinators will find the funding detail useful too.

What is a continence assessment under the NDIS?

A continence assessment is a structured clinical review of bladder and bowel function and, importantly, of how that function affects someone's daily life. It is not simply a form. The assessor looks at medical history, current management strategies, toileting access, mobility, skin integrity and the practical impact on sleeping, working, studying and going out.

The output is a formal written report. That report is what the NDIS uses to decide whether to fund continence products, equipment or supports, and at what level. A vague report produces a vague funding decision, which is why the quality of the assessment matters considerably more than most people expect.

Who can conduct an NDIS continence assessment?

The assessment must be carried out by a suitably qualified health professional. In practice this is most often a registered nurse with continence experience, and sometimes a continence physiotherapist or an occupational therapist working in the area.

What matters is that the assessor can document clinical findings in language an NDIS delegate recognises and can act on. A registered nurse understands the physiology behind bladder and bowel dysfunction, can identify when a presentation needs further medical review, and is accountable to a national regulator through AHPRA registration. That accountability is part of why nurse-written reports carry weight.

What does the assessment involve?

A home visit assessment generally runs 45 to 90 minutes. Being assessed at home rather than in a clinic gives a far more accurate picture, because the assessor can see the actual bathroom, the actual distance from the bed, and the actual equipment in use.

The visit covers a review of medical and continence history, a physical assessment relevant to bladder and bowel function, and a functional assessment of the impact on daily living. The nurse will ask about fluid intake, medication, mobility, night-time routines and what has already been tried. None of it is designed to be uncomfortable, and a participant can have a family member or support worker present throughout.

What is included in the report?

The report documents the assessment findings, sets out the functional impact, and makes specific recommendations. That usually includes continence product types and quantities, any equipment required, and strategies for management. It identifies which NDIS support category the recommendations fall under, and where appropriate it recommends follow-up or referral.

Specificity is what makes a report useful. A report stating that a participant requires a particular product type in a particular quantity per day gives a planner something to fund. A report describing general difficulty does not.

How is an NDIS continence assessment funded?

This is the part that most often surprises people. The assessment itself is funded from Capacity Building, specifically Improved Daily Living, rather than from Core Supports. The continence products and equipment that follow are usually funded separately, generally through Consumables.

That split matters, because a participant can have plenty of Core funding and still be unable to pay for an assessment if Capacity Building is empty. It is worth checking before booking. Assessments are billed at current NDIS Price Guide rates, and for participants with the relevant funding available there is no out-of-pocket cost.

All three plan types can access an assessment. Self-managed and plan-managed participants can use any suitable provider. NDIA-managed participants must use an NDIS registered provider.

Who is eligible?

Any NDIS participant whose bladder or bowel difficulties relate to their disability can have a continence assessment, at any age. It is commonly arranged for participants with spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, cerebral palsy, acquired brain injury, dementia and a range of neurological and developmental conditions.

Eligibility is not the barrier for most people. Knowing that the assessment exists, and that it needs to be arranged rather than waited for, is the barrier.

How to access a continence assessment in Melbourne

Contact a provider directly. No GP referral is required. A support coordinator or plan manager can also arrange it on a participant's behalf, and family members can make the initial enquiry.
