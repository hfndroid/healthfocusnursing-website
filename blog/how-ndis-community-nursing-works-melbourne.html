How NDIS community nursing works in Melbourne

A guide for participants, families and coordinators. What community nursing covers, which budget pays, and how referrals actually work.

Health Focus Nursing · Updated 21 September 2026 · 6 min read

Community nursing is one of the most useful supports in the NDIS and one of the least well understood. Plenty of participants who qualify for it never access it, either because they assume nursing only happens in hospitals, or because nobody told them their plan could fund a nurse to come to the house.

This guide explains what community nursing covers, how it differs from personal care, which budget pays for it, what high-intensity supports actually means, and how a coordinator refers a client.

What is NDIS community nursing?

Community nursing means clinical nursing care delivered in someone's home rather than in a clinic or hospital. A registered nurse travels to the participant, carries out the clinical task, documents it, and coordinates with treating clinicians where needed.

For participants managing catheters, wounds, complex medication routines or high-intensity clinical needs, this removes an enormous amount of friction from an ordinary week. It also reduces infection exposure and, for many people, produces better outcomes simply because recovery happens somewhere familiar.

What does community nursing include?

The scope is broader than most people expect. It covers wound assessment and dressing, catheter insertion and management across in-dwelling, in-out and suprapubic types, medication administration and monitoring, bowel management programs and stoma care, diabetes monitoring and education, and overnight clinical attendance where monitoring is required.

It also covers continence assessment, and transition care for participants moving from hospital back home, which is the period when complications are most likely and support is most often missing.

Who needs NDIS community nursing?

Participants with complex or changing health needs, people recently discharged from hospital, those managing chronic conditions requiring regular clinical review, and participants with tracheostomies, ventilators, feeding tubes or severe dysphagia.

It is also frequently the right support where a family carer has been performing clinical tasks themselves. That situation is more common than it should be, and it is usually unsustainable.

How is community nursing funded under the NDIS?

Community nursing is funded under NDIS support item 0114, Community Nursing Care, which sits within Core Supports. It is billed at current NDIS Price Guide rates.

All three plan management types can access it. Self-managed and plan-managed participants can choose any suitable provider. NDIA-managed participants must use an NDIS registered provider, which makes provider registration a practical gating factor rather than a formality.

Note that nursing and personal care come from different items. Personal care sits at 0107 and covers non-clinical daily living support delivered by support workers. Many participants receive both, and the distinction matters when a plan is being built.

High-intensity supports, explained

High-intensity daily personal activities are supports requiring advanced clinical training and registered nurse oversight. The category includes complex wound management, urinary catheter management, complex bowel care, enteral feeding via naso-gastric, jejunum and duodenum routes, tracheostomy management, ventilator management, subcutaneous injections and severe dysphagia management.

Two things are required to access these: funding in the plan, and a provider holding the specific NDIS registration to deliver them. Not every provider does, and this is worth confirming before a service agreement is signed rather than after.

How to access community nursing in Melbourne

Contact a registered provider directly. No GP referral is required for NDIS-funded nursing. The provider will arrange an initial assessment, usually a home visit by a registered nurse, and build a care plan against the funding available.

Ask two questions early: is there a waitlist, and can the provider reliably service your suburb. Both determine whether care actually starts or simply gets scheduled.

How support coordinators refer clients

Coordinators refer by phone, email or a provider referral form. A useful referral includes the participant's first name and suburb, the service required, the plan type, and the coordinator's contact details. Detailed clinical information is helpful but not essential at referral stage, since a registered nurse completes a full assessment at the first visit.

What coordinators should expect back is an acknowledgement within one business day and a service agreement within 48 hours. Health Focus Nursing works to both, and coordinators receive progress updates throughout. You can refer directly through our coordinator page, or read more about our community nursing service.
